# Why learn data science if AI can already do the work?

![[images/competing-with-ai.png|Robot and human working side by side|1100]]

I’ve decided to learn data science. Python, SQL, statistics, machine learning in my free time, but I am struggling with something with the necessity. Even if I spend the next year learning all those technologies I could never be as good as AI. Even today, AI can do create code and setup entire sites. In fact, this quartz site was setup using AI in like 5 minutes(obviuously lol)

I am not new to tech, I have dabbled with programming, I even know a little LUA and Python. Being in community for so long I have seen how fast tools change. One area that I suck at is math. We have never really been on speaking terms outside of budgeting my coffee addiction; the idea of spending hours and tutorial after tutorial sounds like pain. Not with GTA VI coming up and WoW Forever sitting on my hard drive and especially since I am tell an AI model what I want and get basically a work script in a few minutes or even seconds. 

So am I just doing this wrong? Am I wasting my time?

## Do I need to become a programmer?

Ok so let's say I spend all this time learning all this stuff. Building projects, doing tutorials you know, the fun stuff. I guess I can say that I put my mind to something and learned a new skill, hooray! But where will AI be in a year?

Right now it can write scripts, queries, charts, and help with model training. Things that take me hours can take it minutes, and it’s only going to get better. Trying to “out-code” that feels like a bad bet.

I still think Python and SQL are important. You do need a base level of knowledge in order to know when it's telling or doing something you don't want it to. But maybe I need to change my goal, maybe I need to look at these technologies as tools rather than the end all be all. Similiar to how AI is a tool. That would certainly save me from having to memorize every function. 

## AI likes to do things, even when it shouldn’t

One thing I notice is that AI can be too helpful, even when you don't want it to be. 

I have asked it many times to fix a piece of code, and instead of fixing just that code, it rewrote parts of the script that had nothing to do with it. Or it made it so unreadable that I coudn't even tell what the hell I was looking at anymore. 

Apparently, I'm not the only one that has noticed that. A 2026 paper, [Coding Agents Don’t Know When to Act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07769), looked at how AI coding agents handle software issues that were already resolved. The researchers built a set of 200 tasks where the right move was to change nothing, then tested five models across four agent setups.

Even the stronger agents suggested unnecessary changes 35% to 65% of the time. The paper calls this *action bias*: the model acts when leaving things alone would have been better.

Doesn't mean that AI is bad; it means that just because it gave me an answer, doesn't mean the answer was necessary. Be like talking to your partner and they want to fix the sink even though the front door is the problem haha.

I keep wondering how often that shows up in data work. What if it finds a pattern that isn’t real? What if the data is incomplete and it still sounds sure of itself? If I don’t know enough about data science, how would I catch that?

## Learning to think about the data

So I think I want to shift my thinking a bit. If programming is just the ability to problem solve and translate that into actionable code, then what if I took the time to learn how to think like a data scientist?

Not by prompting ChatGPT with “you’re an expert data scientist with 20 years of experience.” That just gives it a fake resume. It still doesn’t know the business, the people, how the data was collected, or what’s missing unless you tell it. And even then, it can still be wrong.

I want to get better at questions like:

- Where did this data come from, and can I trust it?
- Are we measuring the right things?
- What’s missing that could change the conclusion?
- Is this a real relationship, or noise?
- Are we mixing up correlation and causation?
- What assumptions are baked in?
- If this analysis is wrong, what happens?

Having numbers is easy. Knowing what they mean is the part I care about.

## Slop in, slop out

Old line in computing: garbage in, garbage out. With AI, I’d say **slop in, slop out.**

AI can make bad information look clean. Nice charts, clear explanations, reports that sound fine. Polish doesn’t make the data good.

Say support tickets go down. AI says the new knowledge base is working. Maybe. Or maybe the company lost customers. Or tickets got categorized differently. Or people gave up on support. Same chart. Different story.

If nobody checks the source or challenges the conclusion, you can end up celebrating the wrong thing.

AI can help spot those problems too, if you give it context and push back. Someone still has to know what to ask. I’d like that someone to be me.

## Am I still learning Python and SQL?

Yes. I’m not skipping the tools.

I still want Python, SQL, stats, machine learning, and the basics. I’m just treating them as tools for working with data, not as the whole point.

I want enough skill to read code, spot problems, and change things when I need to. I want enough math that I don’t trust a result just because a computer printed it.

And I’m going to use AI while I learn. Pretending it doesn’t exist while I slowly reinvent what it already does seems silly. There’s a difference between using AI to learn and using it to skip learning. I’ll probably forget that difference more than once.

## First experiment

Instead of writing more about what I think AI can and can’t do, I want to try something concrete.

I’m going to take a dataset that I already have in mind and put AI to the test. There's an audit I've been wanting to do on our community around how effectively we're answering customer questions. The idea comes from a [blog written by Richard Millington](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/measuring-call-deflection-has-held-communities-back-years-millington-kokre/) on measuring case deflection through online communities.

Basically, the short version is this: Of all the support cases that come in, how many could have been answered using publicly available knowledge, such as help documentation? Then, how many could have been answered using that same documentation **plus answers from the community?**

The difference between those two numbers gives us a pretty solid baseline for understanding how much additional value the community brings to self-service.

I already have a really good understanding of the data and what I'm trying to measure, so I'm particularly interested in seeing how AI approaches the analysis. I will be curious if it finds the same conclusions or identifies gaps. 

Either way, I'll be documenting the journey and sharing what I learn along the way.
